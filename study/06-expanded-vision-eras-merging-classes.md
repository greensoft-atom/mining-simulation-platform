I think your vision is becoming much clearer. You are not just designing a mining game. You are aiming to create a **long-running online mining RPG with a living economy, deep progression trees, recognizable heroes, social groups, strategic competition, and a business model that can grow for years.**

The key is to build a game that can keep adding new possibilities without constantly rebuilding its foundations. Players should be able to join for different reasons—collecting, exploration, crafting, strategy, social leadership, competition, or conquest—and still feel that they belong to the same world.

I agree with most of your ideas. I would refine one important point: **paying players should be able to accelerate their progress and gain meaningful advantages, but the game should not become a contest where the biggest spender automatically defeats everyone else.** You want high spenders to remain invested, while skilled newcomers, dedicated free players, and organized guilds still have reasons to compete.

Here is how I would develop your ideas into a more ambitious, expandable design.

# 1. Build a game world that can grow through multiple eras

Rather than using player level as the only measure of progression, I recommend a combination of **levels, eras, professions, specializations, and achievements**.

## A. The Era System

Players and civilizations advance through technological or magical eras. Each era introduces new resources, crafting possibilities, enemies, locations, and strategic choices.

| Era                         | Theme                      | New possibilities                                                       |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Settlement Era           | Primitive mining camps     | Basic mining, wood, stone, simple tools, small workshops                |
| 2. Bronze Era               | Early industry             | Smelting, better equipment, specialist workers, organized expeditions   |
| 3. Iron Kingdoms            | Fortifications and warfare | Armor, siege equipment, defensive structures, guild outposts            |
| 4. Crystal Era              | Arcane discoveries         | Magic skills, crystal-powered machines, enchanted equipment             |
| 5. Steam Era                | Industrial expansion       | Automated production, transport networks, advanced workshops            |
| 6. Ancient Civilization Era | Lost technology            | Ancient ruins, relic research, rare bosses, complex crafting            |
| 7. Astral Era               | Endgame exploration        | Extraordinary materials, dimensional mines, high-level group challenges |

These are illustrative eras, not a commitment to a particular fantasy setting. We could choose a more distinctive theme later.

### The critical design decision: do not make earlier eras worthless

If a player reaches the Crystal Era, they should still need something produced by earlier-era players.

For example:

- Bronze-era miners produce alloys used in specialist tools.
- Iron-era crafters manufacture components for crystal machinery.
- Crystal-era explorers discover materials needed by advanced researchers.
- Veteran engineers manufacture equipment used by newer players.
- Traders connect these different groups.

This gives older content a continuing economic purpose.

**My recommendation:** Introduce new eras through expansions and seasonal world developments, rather than continuously increasing everyone's level cap. New eras should add new decisions, not simply make all existing equipment obsolete.

## B. Four different kinds of advancement

Instead of one enormous level number, give players four complementary progression paths:

1. **Character progression:** levels, attributes, combat abilities and equipment.
2. **Profession progression:** mining, refining, crafting, exploration, trading and other specialties.
3. **Civilization progression:** workshops, estates, research, infrastructure and territory development.
4. **Prestige progression:** long-term achievements, titles, rare appearances, collections and prestigious unlocks.

A player might have a modest combat level but become a famous master craftsman. Another might be an elite combatant whose greatest achievement is leading a successful guild.

Both should have a reason to keep playing.

# 2. Make merging a major progression system—not just a simple upgrade button

Your merging idea has significant potential because it combines collection, accumulation, discovery and long-term goals.

However, I would avoid making every part of the game a merge mechanic. Merging should connect several systems while preserving the value of mining, crafting, combat and trading.

## A. The basic merging loop

For example:

- 5 Raw Copper → 1 Refined Copper
- 5 Refined Copper → 1 Copper Component
- 5 Copper Components → 1 Advanced Component
- Advanced Components + a rare blueprint → a specialist tool

The numbers are illustrative; actual recipes must be balanced through testing.

The more interesting version lets players choose different directions.

A refined copper component might become:

- A mining tool component.
- A defensive armor component.
- A weapon component.
- A workshop machine component.
- A trade good.

This creates decisions instead of requiring everyone to follow the same upgrade path.

## B. Build branching evolution trees

Imagine a rare mineral called **Starstone**.

A player could develop it into one of three branches:

**Industrial branch**

- Efficient processing
- Higher production capacity
- Automated mining equipment

**Combat branch**

- Weapon enhancements
- Armor penetration
- Siege equipment

**Arcane branch**

- Magical attacks
- Defensive barriers
- Exploration abilities

Later, advanced recipes might combine products from multiple branches. That gives guilds a reason to cooperate with specialists rather than expecting every member to master everything.

## C. Protect the value of rare discoveries

Merging has a potential economic problem: if every item can be combined into something better, players may eventually flood the game with high-tier items.

I would introduce several item categories:

- **Common materials:** predictable, plentiful and consumed in normal production.
- **Rare materials:** obtained through difficult mines, bosses and exploration.
- **Blueprints:** unlock particular crafting possibilities.
- **Relics:** provide unusual abilities or modify a build.
- **Unique discoveries:** scarce, collectible objects with prestige or specialized uses.

Not every item should be mergeable. Some should be useful because they are rare, historically significant, or difficult to obtain again.

### An important rule

**Merging should create new possibilities, not merely bigger numbers.**

If every upgrade only increases damage by another 10%, the tree will eventually feel repetitive. More interesting upgrades change how players mine, fight, defend, explore, craft or lead.

# 3. Create distinctive heroes, classes and professions

I agree with your idea that players should have identifiable characteristics and different skills that counter one another.

I would design the character system around **combat roles, economic professions and leadership specializations**. These are connected, but they should not be identical.

A miner does not have to be a warrior. A powerful warrior does not automatically have the best mining skills. A wealthy trader does not necessarily lead a successful army.

This creates a more interesting society.

## A. Combat classes

Here is an initial class roster.

| Class           | Main role           | Strengths                                  | Weaknesses                                      |
| --------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Guardian        | Frontline defense   | Shields, protection, holding chokepoints   | Limited mobility                                |
| Berserker       | Close-range offense | Heavy damage, breaking enemy lines         | Vulnerable to control and ranged attacks        |
| Ranger          | Long-range offense  | Precision, traps, scouting                 | Weak when surrounded                            |
| Assassin        | Infiltration        | Ambushes, disabling key targets            | Vulnerable in prolonged direct combat           |
| Elementalist    | Magical offense     | Area damage, elemental effects             | Resource-intensive; vulnerable when interrupted |
| Warden          | Defensive magic     | Barriers, resistance, battlefield control  | Lower direct damage                             |
| Engineer        | Tactical support    | Turrets, mines, barricades, repair         | Depends on preparation and resources            |
| Medic/Alchemist | Support             | Healing, cleansing, temporary enhancements | Limited offensive power                         |
| Beastmaster     | Summoning           | Pets, tracking, harassment                 | Depends on pet management                       |

This is a starting framework, not a final list. We should test a smaller number of classes before committing to all nine.

## B. Give every class a branching skill tree

For example, a Guardian might specialize in:

- **Bulwark:** protects allies and holds defensive positions.
- **Vanguard:** leads assaults and breaks enemy formations.
- **Sentinel:** counters ranged attacks and protects strategic objectives.

A Ranger might specialize in:

- Long-range precision.
- Traps and area denial.
- Reconnaissance and target marking.

The objective is to give players meaningful choices without requiring them to create a completely new character every time they want to try another role.

## C. Use counters, but avoid absolute counters

An ideal combat system has a relationship like this:

- Guardians help protect Elementalists.
- Elementalists punish clustered enemies.
- Rangers threaten exposed spellcasters.
- Assassins can disrupt Rangers.
- Engineers create obstacles that limit enemy movement.
- Wardens help teams survive magical attacks.

But no class should automatically defeat another simply because of its class label.

Player skill, equipment, positioning, timing, team composition and strategic preparation should all matter.

**A useful principle:** A well-played lower-powered team should sometimes defeat a stronger team, but it should not routinely defeat one with a large power advantage.

That gives skilled players opportunities without making progression and spending meaningless.

# 4. Separate combat power from economic power

This is one of the most important architectural decisions for the game.

Consider two players.

**Player A**

- Excellent fighter.
- Average miner.
- Poor trader.
- Specializes in defeating bosses.

**Player B**

- Average fighter.
- Master craftsman.
- Owns productive workshops.
- Supplies equipment to an entire guild.

Player B should have a meaningful position in the world even if they cannot defeat Player A in a duel.

I recommend six major career paths:

| Career   | Main activity                        | Long-term achievement                            |
| -------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Explorer | Discover mines, secrets and relics   | Rare discoveries and exploration records         |
| Miner    | Extract and specialize in resources  | Access to valuable deposits and mining mastery   |
| Crafter  | Produce tools, armor and machinery   | Renowned products and crafting mastery           |
| Trader   | Connect supply and demand            | Market reputation and commercial influence       |
| Warrior  | Fight monsters and rival players     | Combat rankings and battle achievements          |
| Leader   | Organize teams, guilds and territory | Strategic reputation and leadership achievements |

Players can combine careers, but specialization should offer efficiency, exclusive knowledge or distinctive abilities.

### Make specialization valuable without creating permanent barriers

A new player should be able to become a renowned crafter, trader or tactical leader without first spending a year catching up in combat.

Possible routes to recognition include:

- Discovering a previously unknown mineral deposit.
- Producing the first item from a new recipe.
- Winning a tournament through exceptional tactics.
- Completing a difficult expedition with a small team.
- Building a highly productive workshop.
- Leading a guild to victory.
- Finding an alternative strategy that becomes popular across the server.

These are different paths to fame, and each can support its own leaderboard or recognition system.

# 5. Design combat for solo players, small teams and large-scale competition

I would not begin by implementing every combat mode. Instead, build a common combat foundation and add modes that reuse it.

## A. Three combat modes

| Mode              |             Players | Purpose                                                   |
| ----------------- | ------------------: | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Expedition combat | Solo or 2–6 players | Exploration, bosses, rare materials and equipment         |
| Tactical arena    |         Small teams | Competitive skill, class synergy and tournaments          |
| Territory warfare |              Guilds | Strategic control of mines, fortifications and objectives |

The first two modes can establish the combat mechanics. Territory warfare should arrive after the underlying multiplayer, combat and economy systems are reliable.

## B. Combine real-time action with strategic preparation

You mentioned close-range attacks, long-range attacks, magic, offense and defense. Those mechanics can work well in a real-time combat system.

But the strategy should begin before combat.

Guild leaders could select:

- A balanced formation or an aggressive assault formation.
- Equipment and consumable supplies.
- Reconnaissance missions.
- Defensive structures.
- A limited number of siege machines.
- Which objectives to attack and which to protect.

During combat, individual players control their own characters. Between battles, players manage resources, equipment, intelligence and strategic decisions.

This creates meaningful roles for both highly skilled fighters and thoughtful organizers.

### Do we need turn-based combat?

Not necessarily.

I would start with **real-time combat for the main RPG**, then consider turn-based tactical events as a separate mode if testing shows that your audience enjoys slower, more deliberate competition.

Supporting two entirely different combat engines from the beginning would add unnecessary development cost.

# 6. Design property ownership and territorial conflict carefully

Your idea of taking, occupying or stealing other players' properties could produce exciting competition. It could also destroy retention if players lose months of progress while offline.

I recommend three layers of ownership.

## Layer 1: Personal estate

Every player has a personal home, workshop or mining estate.

It contains:

- Permanent progression.
- Equipment storage.
- Production facilities.
- Decorations and trophies.
- Personal collections.
- Upgradeable infrastructure.

Other players can visit it, admire it, trade with its owner or compete against its production records.

**Core rule: another player cannot permanently destroy or steal a player's core estate, purchased cosmetics or accumulated personal collection.**

This provides a safe foundation for casual players and paying customers alike.

## Layer 2: Guild territory

Guilds can compete for:

- Rich mineral deposits.
- Mining outposts.
- Transportation routes.
- Ruins containing research opportunities.
- Special workshops.
- Strategic entrances to dangerous regions.

Controlling a territory should provide useful advantages, but not unlimited access to exclusive resources that make a winning guild impossible to challenge.

Territories could have scheduled conflict windows, defensive preparations, upkeep requirements and rotation mechanisms.

A small guild should still have opportunities to win valuable objectives even when it cannot defeat the largest guild directly.

## Layer 3: Contested resources and limited raids

This is where more aggressive gameplay can exist.

For example:

- Attackers contest a temporary mining operation.
- Defenders protect a resource convoy.
- A guild attempts to occupy a mine for a limited period.
- Players raid a frontier outpost and claim part of its uncollected production.
- A team captures a strategic machine and uses it to gain a temporary advantage.

Rewards can include a limited quantity of exposed resources, temporary control, battle points or territory influence.

Avoid allowing a victorious guild to confiscate a losing player's entire inventory or erase weeks of progression.

### Why this matters commercially

A highly aggressive game can attract committed competitors and substantial spenders. But if weaker players feel they are simply resources for stronger players, they may leave.

You need enough conflict to create excitement and enough security to make long-term investment feel worthwhile.

The ideal is **risk in contested gameplay, security in permanent progression**.

# 7. Help new players become famous—even in an old world

You specifically want new players to have opportunities to reach the top. I think this should be a core design requirement, not an afterthought.

A simple global power leaderboard will naturally favor older players and large spenders. It will become increasingly difficult for newcomers to compete.

Instead, create several different ways to rank and recognize players.

| Ranking          | What it rewards                                    |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Combat champion  | PvP performance and tournament results             |
| Master explorer  | Discoveries and exploration achievements           |
| Master crafter   | Crafting mastery and exceptional products          |
| Market innovator | Trading achievements and economic contribution     |
| Guild strategist | Tactical decisions and objective-based victories   |
| Frontier pioneer | Success in newly released content                  |
| Rising star      | Exceptional achievements by relatively new players |
| Community legend | Long-term contributions to the game community      |

Not every ranking should reward the same behavior.

For example, the Rising Star category could consider achievements relative to a player's progression stage, with safeguards against creating alternate accounts to manipulate rankings.

## Introduce fresh competitive opportunities

When new regions, bosses, crafting systems or seasonal objectives are released, give players opportunities to compete in those environments.

A veteran may have superior equipment, but a clever newcomer could discover a new boss strategy, master a newly introduced class or lead an efficient expedition.

You can also use:

- Weekly tactical challenges.
- Skill-based tournaments.
- Time-limited exploration competitions.
- New-player leagues.
- Seasonal guild objectives.
- Achievement-based titles.

The aim is not to make veterans weak. It is to make the world large enough that **being powerful is not the only way to be important**.

# 8. Monetization: build a business, not just a shop

You want frequent small purchases, larger purchases from competitive players, individual spenders, and organized groups whose leaders may coordinate spending.

That is a reasonable set of commercial objectives. The challenge is making purchases valuable without making the game feel compulsory or unfair.

I would build a layered monetization system.

## A. Individual spending

| Purchase category    | Examples                                                                  | Commercial purpose                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Cosmetics            | Character appearances, armor skins, mining effects                        | Personal expression and identity     |
| Estate customization | Buildings, decorations, themes and visual upgrades                        | Ownership and collection             |
| Seasonal pass        | Rewards for completing seasonal objectives                                | Predictable recurring revenue        |
| Membership           | Additional convenience, customization options and useful account services | Subscription revenue                 |
| Direct purchases     | Clearly priced cosmetic bundles and selected resources                    | Flexible spending levels             |
| Collection packages  | Cosmetic collections, themed objects and display items                    | Completion and collecting motivation |

The exact value of these products must be tested. A membership is only compelling if it offers ongoing value beyond what players can easily obtain elsewhere.

For an ad-free game, avoid making the player feel that the only way to progress comfortably is to purchase a convenience bundle every day.

## B. Competitive spending

Competitive players may spend more than casual collectors, particularly if they identify strongly with a character, guild or competitive identity.

Possible offerings include:

- Unique character appearances.
- Prestige animations and victory effects.
- Guild banners and fortress appearances.
- Tournament entry cosmetics.
- Personalized battle introductions.
- Spectator and replay features.
- Premium seasonal collections.

I would be cautious about selling direct combat power, especially if the same players can buy advantages and then dominate weaker players.

You can offer paid acceleration in carefully limited contexts, but paid advantages should not scale indefinitely with spending.

## C. Guild spending

Your idea of leaders organizing purchases among guild members is worth exploring, but I would implement it as a transparent guild-support system rather than making players feel pressured to pay.

Potential products:

- Guild appearance bundles.
- Shared cosmetic decorations.
- Guild headquarters themes.
- Commemorative statues and banners.
- Guild season passes with collective cosmetic milestones.
- Optional sponsorship of special guild events.

For example, a guild might purchase a visual theme for its headquarters. Members can contribute voluntarily, and the game displays the collective achievement.

The reward is a shared identity—not a permanent advantage that only wealthy guilds can buy.

**Important:** Keep guild purchases voluntary, make the benefits clear, and do not give leaders mechanisms to secretly charge members or transfer their purchased assets.

## D. Should you sell combat power?

Here is my recommendation:

| Monetization type                                      | Recommendation        |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Cosmetic power fantasy                                 | Strongly support      |
| Paid convenience                                       | Support with limits   |
| Faster personal progression                            | Test carefully        |
| Directly purchasable combat strength                   | Restrict              |
| Purchasable permanent territorial dominance            | Avoid                 |
| Paid random rewards                                    | Avoid as a core model |
| Competitive advantages available only to high spenders | Avoid                 |

You mentioned protecting high-paying players so they retain power proportional to their spending.

I would instead protect their **investment, status and identity**. A major spender should receive excellent service, desirable customization, meaningful progression and prestigious recognition. But spending should not guarantee victory over all other players.

This is not merely an ethical consideration. It is a business decision: if spending determines every outcome, competitive players may eventually conclude that skill and strategy no longer matter.

# 9. Give players a reason to return every day

Daily engagement should not mean forcing everyone to complete the same repetitive checklist.

Your game can support several different daily routines.

## A. The daily activity ecosystem

| Player motivation | Example daily activity                                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Collection        | Search for missing minerals, relics or equipment       |
| Progression       | Upgrade tools, merge materials and unlock recipes      |
| Exploration       | Enter a new mine or investigate a mysterious event     |
| Combat            | Complete a boss encounter or tactical challenge        |
| Social play       | Help guild members or join a group expedition          |
| Competition       | Participate in an arena match or leaderboard challenge |
| Economy           | Craft orders, manage workshops or trade resources      |
| Ownership         | Customize an estate or improve production              |
| Leadership        | Assign expedition roles and prepare a guild objective  |

These activities should share the same economy. A crafter's work should support expeditions; expeditions should supply rare crafting materials; guilds should create demand for both.

## B. Use a flexible daily structure

I would offer three activity levels.

**Short session: 3–5 minutes**

- Collect completed production.
- Review a market order.
- Claim a completed expedition reward.
- Make one meaningful upgrade.

**Medium session: 10–20 minutes**

- Explore a mine.
- Craft a new item.
- Complete a combat encounter.
- Help a guild member.

**Long session: 30–60 minutes**

- Participate in a major expedition.
- Join a tournament.
- Coordinate a guild operation.
- Explore a challenging new area.

These are design targets, not guaranteed session lengths. Testing will reveal what your audience actually prefers.

### Do not punish players for missing a day

A player who takes a three-day break should not return to find their entire estate destroyed.

Use reasonable offline production, catch-up opportunities, flexible seasonal objectives and rotating activities.

This supports long-term participation without turning the game into an obligation.

# 10. Create recurring events that evolve over time

Daily tasks alone cannot sustain a live game for years. You need a calendar of activities with different rhythms.

Here is a possible structure.

| Frequency              | Event                                      | Main purpose                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Daily                  | Mine discoveries and rotating challenges   | Short-term engagement          |
| Several times per week | Group expeditions and boss encounters      | Cooperative play               |
| Weekly                 | Guild objectives and tactical competitions | Social coordination            |
| Monthly                | Territory campaigns and special events     | Strategic competition          |
| Every 2–3 months       | Major seasonal theme and progression       | Re-engagement and monetization |
| Several times per year | New region, era or major system            | Long-term expansion            |

These frequencies should be adjusted after observing retention, team capacity and event participation.

## A particularly useful idea: the world discovery system

Imagine a server-wide event where miners begin discovering an unfamiliar crystal.

At first, nobody knows its purpose.

As players collect samples:

1. Explorers discover clues.
2. Miners gather the material.
3. Crafters experiment with recipes.
4. Researchers unlock a new technology.
5. Guilds compete to control the region.
6. The server community unlocks a new area or boss.

The discovery becomes a shared story. Different player groups contribute in different ways.

That creates a reason to participate beyond obtaining a daily reward.

It also provides a repeatable structure for future content: introduce a mystery, distribute discoveries among professions, let players investigate, and reveal a new part of the world.

# 11. Expand the game without creating an unmanageable development burden

You want a flexible game with many trees and opportunities. That is good—but there is a difference between a game that can expand and a game that tries to launch with everything.

I would design the architecture around modular systems.

## A. Systems that should be designed for expansion

- Character classes and skill trees.
- Item types, rarity and crafting recipes.
- Mining regions and environmental rules.
- Professions and specializations.
- Era-specific resources.
- Monsters, bosses and encounter mechanics.
- Guild roles and territory objectives.
- Seasonal events and reward tracks.
- Market rules and resource sinks.
- Cosmetic categories.
- Achievements and rankings.

For example, a new era should ideally be introduced by configuring new resources, recipes, enemies, areas and objectives within existing systems—not rewriting the core game.

Likewise, a new character class should be added through a consistent ability framework, rather than requiring a unique implementation for every skill.

## B. Separate permanent progression from seasonal competition

Permanent progression includes:

- Character development.
- Collections and discoveries.
- Estate development.
- Crafting mastery.
- Account achievements.

Seasonal competition includes:

- Territory campaigns.
- Temporary rankings.
- Seasonal challenges.
- Limited-time objectives.
- Special event rewards.

This combination gives players a persistent identity while allowing the competitive environment to refresh.

## C. Do not create a new era merely to make old equipment useless

New content should expand the decision space.

For example, a new material might be exceptionally good for magical resistance but poor for physical defense. Another might enable fast mining but be fragile in combat.

This gives existing equipment a continued role and reduces the pressure to inflate every statistic with each expansion.

# 12. Give each player group its own reason to stay

Your audience will not be a single homogeneous group. I would explicitly design for these communities:

| Community            | What attracts them                      | What keeps them engaged                                  |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Collectors           | Rare items, completion and discovery    | New collections, displays and prestige                   |
| Min-maxers           | Efficient builds and optimized systems  | New combinations and balance challenges                  |
| Casual builders      | Relaxed production and customization    | New structures and flexible progression                  |
| Competitive fighters | Combat skill and ranking                | Tournaments and meaningful rivals                        |
| Traders              | Supply, demand and profitable decisions | New materials, recipes and market opportunities          |
| Guild leaders        | Organization and strategic influence    | Territory objectives and group achievements              |
| Social players       | Friends and shared experiences          | Group expeditions and community events                   |
| High spenders        | Expression, progression and recognition | Premium content, excellent service and meaningful status |
| Newcomers            | Discovery and attainable goals          | Catch-up systems and fresh competitions                  |

The important part is that these groups should interact economically and socially, without all needing to enjoy the same activity.

A player who dislikes PvP should still be able to contribute to a guild through production, logistics or research. A player who dislikes crafting should still have access to useful equipment through trading and group play.

# 13. Additional ideas I think could make the game distinctive

These are the features I would seriously consider for your long-term roadmap.

### Idea 1: The player-discovered world

Let players discover rare locations, hidden passages, abandoned civilizations and unusual deposits.

Some discoveries could be personal; others could be announced server-wide.

The first explorer to discover a region might earn a permanent title, while other players could still explore it later.

### Idea 2: Player-created equipment identities

Allow crafters to attach a maker's mark to eligible equipment.

A famous crafter's products could become desirable because of their quality, appearance, history or special properties.

This gives crafting its own prestige economy.

Be careful not to make every item permanently traceable or tradeable if doing so complicates moderation, duplication prevention or balance.

### Idea 3: Guild research trees

Guilds could specialize in different areas:

- Industrial production.
- Exploration and surveying.
- Defensive engineering.
- Magical research.
- Trade and logistics.
- Combat coordination.

A guild would not be able to maximize everything immediately. Leaders would need to decide which capabilities matter most.

This creates strategic identity without requiring every guild to follow the same build.

### Idea 4: The world changes because of player activity

Suppose players extract too much ore from one region. The area becomes less productive, forcing expeditions to explore deeper or discover new deposits.

Alternatively, a guild might build a transport network that improves regional trade.

These changes should be controlled and understandable. You do not want players to feel that their favorite mining location has been arbitrarily ruined.

### Idea 5: Player-run expeditions

Let a player act as an expedition leader.

They choose a destination, select a difficulty, recruit specialists and distribute rewards according to transparent rules established before departure.

The leader's reputation depends on successful expeditions and fair management.

This creates social opportunities and leadership gameplay without requiring the leader to be the strongest fighter.

### Idea 6: A server-wide museum or Hall of Fame

Display extraordinary discoveries, tournament winners, historic guild achievements and legendary crafted items.

Players could visit, inspect achievements and see the history of their server.

This gives prestige a persistent, visible form—and creates a long-term reason to pursue rare achievements beyond their immediate statistical benefits.

### Idea 7: Controlled player contracts

Players and guilds could post requests for materials, crafting services, exploration or combat assistance.

Contracts should have transparent rewards, deadlines and completion conditions. The game should handle escrow and settlement where appropriate to prevent scams.

This creates economic and social interactions that can scale as the player base grows.

### Idea 8: A strategic intelligence profession

Scouting could become a meaningful role rather than a minor button.

Scouts might reveal:

- Approximate resource deposits.
- Enemy defensive structures.
- Dangerous routes.
- Boss weaknesses.
- Territory activity.

Information should be useful but imperfect, so it creates strategic decisions rather than eliminating uncertainty entirely.

# 14. The business risks we should address early

A broad, ambitious game can fail even when its individual ideas are good. These are the problems I would plan for from the start.

| Risk                    | Why it matters                                                  | Mitigation                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Too much scope          | A four-person team cannot implement every system simultaneously | Launch a narrow but expandable foundation                                         |
| Power inflation         | New items make older content irrelevant                         | Use specialization and situational trade-offs                                     |
| Wealth concentration    | Veteran guilds control the economy and territory                | Rotate opportunities, maintain alternative routes and control resource monopolies |
| Pay-to-win              | Spending overwhelms skill                                       | Bound purchased advantages and protect competitive integrity                      |
| Resource inflation      | Players accumulate more currency than they can use              | Create useful, balanced resource sinks                                            |
| New-player disadvantage | Newcomers see no realistic path to success                      | New-player competitions, fresh content and alternative fame paths                 |
| Daily fatigue           | Players feel obligated to log in                                | Flexible objectives and reasonable offline progression                            |
| Exploits and bots       | Cheating undermines the economy                                 | Server-authoritative validation, transaction auditing and anomaly detection       |
| Guild pressure          | Members feel forced to spend or play                            | Voluntary contributions and clear rules                                           |
| Content exhaustion      | Players finish the available progression                        | Add systems that generate new combinations, not just more levels                  |

For the economy, we should specifically track how much currency and material enters the world, where it goes, how quickly it circulates and which players control the largest reserves.

For monetization, track retention and spending together. If revenue rises briefly while long-term retention collapses, that is a warning rather than a success.

# 15. How I would organize the first year

The following is a development sequence, not a promise that every phase can be completed on a fixed schedule.

### Phase 1: Prove the core loop

Build:

- One mine.
- A small set of materials.
- Mining and extraction.
- Basic crafting and merging.
- A few upgrade branches.
- One character class.
- One personal estate.
- One enemy or environmental challenge.
- Persistent account and inventory data.

The key question: **Is mining, discovering, upgrading and crafting satisfying even before the large multiplayer systems exist?**

### Phase 2: Prove that players return

Add:

- Several classes or specializations.
- A deeper progression tree.
- Multiple mining areas.
- A small boss encounter.
- Collection and achievement systems.
- Daily and weekly objectives.
- A limited set of direct purchases and cosmetics.

Test whether players return because they genuinely want to progress—not just because rewards expire.

### Phase 3: Prove multiplayer

Add:

- Group expeditions.
- Shared boss encounters.
- Guild creation.
- Guild research.
- Trading or controlled player contracts.
- Basic competitive rankings.

At this stage, test cooperation, competition, cheating, economic exploits and server performance.

### Phase 4: Prove the territorial game

Only after the earlier systems work well should we introduce:

- Claimable territories.
- Defensive structures.
- Scheduled battles.
- Temporary resource raids.
- Guild campaigns.
- Strategic scouting and logistics.

### Phase 5: Expand the live world

Use actual player behavior to determine the next major addition.

It might be a new era, more professions, a player marketplace, deeper combat, new classes or additional territory mechanics.

**The best next feature is not necessarily the most exciting feature on paper. It is the one that improves the game your players are actually playing.**

# 16. My strongest recommendation: establish five design principles now

Before we build the competitor database, I would adopt five principles as the foundation for every future decision.

**1. Infinite-looking progression, finite development complexity.**  
Players should see enormous possibilities through branching systems and combinations, while the development team reuses reliable frameworks.

**2. Multiple forms of power.**  
Combat strength, wealth, knowledge, production, leadership and reputation should all matter. No single number should determine a player's entire value.

**3. Risk without destroying investment.**  
Territory and temporary resources can be contested. Permanent progression, personal identity and purchased possessions need strong protection.

**4. Spending buys value, not guaranteed supremacy.**  
Paying players deserve meaningful benefits, but skill, strategy, teamwork and economic expertise must remain relevant.

**5. Every new system should create connections.**  
A new resource should connect to professions, recipes, exploration or competition. A new class should create team combinations. A new region should introduce new strategic and economic opportunities.

This fifth principle is especially important. It allows the game to become richer over time rather than simply larger.

# 17. Three decisions I would settle with you next

I would hold off on the 30–50-game competitor database until we agree on these. They influence the entire design, including server architecture, development cost, monetization and long-term operations.

### Decision A: What should the player actually control?

**Option A — One hero:**  
Players primarily develop one character, with equipment, classes and skills.

**Option B — A hero plus a team:**  
Players develop a main hero and recruit additional characters, workers or companions who perform different roles.

**Option C — A hero plus a growing mining civilization:**  
Players develop their hero, recruit specialists, build a personal estate, manage production and eventually contribute to a guild's larger economy.

**My recommendation: Option C**, provided that the civilization-management layer stays manageable. It supports collecting, merging, RPG combat, idle production, leadership and long-term expansion.

### Decision B: How should progression work across eras?

**Option A — Everyone progresses through the same eras.**  
Easy to understand and balance, but players may feel forced to follow a single path.

**Option B — Players specialize in different technological or magical branches.**  
More strategic, with stronger identities and trade between specialists.

**Option C — A shared world era with personal specialization.**  
The server progresses through major world developments, while each player chooses their own professional and technological branches.

**My recommendation: Option C.** It combines a shared history with individual freedom. It also makes server-wide discoveries and live events more meaningful.

### Decision C: How much advantage should spending provide?

**Option A — Mostly cosmetic spending.**  
Strong competitive fairness, but the commercial model must be supported by desirable customization, collections and recurring content.

**Option B — Cosmetics, convenience and limited progression acceleration.**  
A wider range of monetization, with more balancing work required.

**Option C — Significant purchasable combat power.**  
Potentially higher spending from competitive players, but also greater risk of unfair competition, player churn and guild dominance.

**My recommendation: Option B**, with a clear ceiling on purchased competitive advantages and no permanent pay-to-own-the-world system.

---

**If I were making this game with your four-person team, I would aim for Option C in both player control and era progression, and Option B for monetization.**

That gives us the broadest foundation for a persistent mining RPG with recognizable heroes, branching merge trees, personal estates, guilds, territory competition, a living economy and a long-term live-service business.

Once we settle those three decisions, we can build the competitor database around the specific systems we want to learn from—not merely collect a list of similar games.
